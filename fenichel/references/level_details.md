# Bloom's Taxonomy — Level Details

Reference for classifying a student's question about a given source (text, image, or other stimulus). For each level: what the student must do, common cue verbs (a starting point, not a checklist to pattern-match against), and a worked example using the same hypothetical source so the contrast is clear.

**Hypothetical source** (used in every example below): a short paragraph explaining that plants make food through photosynthesis, that chlorophyll in leaves absorbs sunlight, and that this process releases oxygen as a byproduct.

## 1. Remember
The answer is stated directly in the source. The student locates or recalls it — no interpretation required.
- Cue verbs: define, list, name, identify, when, who, what is
- Example: "What is the green pigment in leaves called?" — the source says "chlorophyll" outright.

## 2. Understand
The student restates, explains, or exemplifies the idea in their own words — demonstrating comprehension without adding anything new.
- Cue verbs: explain, summarize, describe, paraphrase, give an example of
- Example: "Can you explain in your own words how plants make their food?" — requires grasping the process, not just quoting a term.

## 3. Apply
The student takes a concept or procedure from the source and uses it in a new, concrete situation the source doesn't itself describe.
- Cue verbs: use, demonstrate, solve, show how, apply this to
- Example: "If a plant was kept in a dark closet for a week, what would happen to it and why?" — applies the source's concept to a scenario it never mentions.

## 4. Analyze
The student breaks the source into parts and examines relationships — causes, comparisons, structure, or assumptions the source doesn't spell out as a single stated fact.
- Cue verbs: compare, contrast, categorize, what's the relationship between, why does X lead to Y
- Example: "How does the amount of sunlight affect how much oxygen a plant produces?" — the source implies a causal chain but never states this relationship directly; the student has to connect two separate facts.

## 5. Evaluate
The student makes and defends a judgment — there's a "which is better" or "do you agree" at the core of it, and a reasonable answer requires a stance plus justification.
- Cue verbs: judge, justify, argue, critique, do you agree, which is more important
- Example: "Is it fair to say plants 'create' energy, or is that a misleading way to describe photosynthesis?" — asks the student to take and defend a position.

## 6. Create
The student combines ideas from the source into something original — a design, a hypothesis, a new system or scenario.
- Cue verbs: design, propose, invent, what would happen if you combined, come up with a way to
- Example: "Design an experiment to test whether the color of light affects how much oxygen a plant produces." — asks the student to build something new using the source's concepts as raw material.

## Edge cases

**Compound questions.** "What is chlorophyll and why is it important?" mixes Remember and Understand. Name both, but classify at the higher level the question is ultimately driving toward — a teacher wants to know the real ceiling of the question, not its floor.

**Vague or off-topic questions.** If a question can't actually be answered from the source and isn't really building on it, say so plainly rather than guessing a level. Something like "this isn't really answerable from the passage — it's a tangent about X" is a more useful response than a forced classification.

**Verb doesn't match operation.** "What do you think would happen if the leaves were blue instead of green?" *looks* like Apply because of "what would happen," but is really asking the student to reason about a hypothetical change to the mechanism — that's Analyze (or Create, if it asks them to redesign something around the change). Always check what the student has to actually do, not just which verb appears.

**Multiple valid sources say different things.** If a question could reasonably sit at two adjacent levels depending on how literally the source states something, pick the level that best matches how directly the source addresses it, and mention the alternative in one clause rather than hedging the whole answer.
